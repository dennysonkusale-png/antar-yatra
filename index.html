The three companions

What is Meditation, Japa and Tap?

Three streams of the same river. Meditation stills the mind; japa gives it a sacred sound to rest in; tap is the steady heat of daily discipline that ripens both. Together they carry a seeker from a restless first sitting to deep, abiding peace.

Dhyana

Meditation

The gentle art of resting attention on one anchor — the breath, a sensation, a flame — and returning, again and again, without judgement. It is less about stopping thoughts than about no longer being ruled by them.

Japa

Mantra Repetition

The rhythmic repetition of a sacred sound or name, often counted on a 108-bead mala. The mind is given a single, holy syllable to hold, and in that holding it grows quiet and absorbed.

Tap

Disciplined Practice

Tap (from tapas, 'heat') is the joyful austerity of showing up daily — same time, same place, same sincere intent. It is not self-punishment but the warmth that matures a practice into a way of being.

Raja Yoga · Patanjali

Ashtanga Yoga — The Eight Limbs

Patanjali's Yoga Sutras describe eight limbs (ashtanga) that carry a seeker from ethical living, through body and breath, into the deepest meditation. They are not eight separate paths but one unfolding discipline.

1

YamaRestraints

The five vows that harmonise our life with the world: non-violence, truthfulness, non-stealing, moderation and non-possessiveness.

Ahimsa — non-violenceSatya — truthfulnessAsteya — non-stealingBrahmacharya — moderationAparigraha — non-possessiveness
2

NiyamaObservances

The five observances that cultivate inner clarity: purity, contentment, disciplined zeal, self-study and surrender to the Divine.

Shaucha — puritySantosha — contentmentTapas — disciplined zealSvadhyaya — self-studyIshvara Pranidhana — surrender
3

AsanaSteady seat

A posture that is both stable and comfortable, so the body no longer disturbs the mind. See the Postures section for how to sit.

4

PranayamaBreath regulation

Slowing and refining the breath to steady the life-force and quiet the mind.

5

PratyaharaWithdrawal of the senses

Turning the senses inward, away from outer distraction, so attention rests within.

6

DharanaConcentration

Gathering the mind onto a single point or object and holding it there.

7

DhyanaMeditation

Sustained, effortless attention in which the observer and the observed draw close.

8

SamadhiAbsorption

Complete absorption, where the sense of separation dissolves and only stillness remains.

The first five limbs — yama, niyama, asana, pranayama and pratyahara — prepare the body and mind. The last three — dharana, dhyana and samadhi — are the inner journey, and together they are called samyama.

03

Step by step

How to Do Meditation

There is no perfect sitting — only a sincere one. Follow these simple steps, and know that every return of attention, no matter how often, is the practice itself.

1

Choose a time and a place

Early morning (the 'brahma muhurta') and dusk are traditionally favoured, when the world is quiet. Pick a corner you will return to daily — a small altar, a cushion, a shawl. Consistency of place quietly trains the mind.

2

Settle the body

Sit in any stable, dignified posture: cross-legged on a cushion, on a chair with feet flat, or kneeling. Keep the spine upright but not rigid, shoulders soft, hands resting in the lap. If sitting is painful, lie down with awareness — or walk slowly.

3

Close the eyes and arrive

Let the eyes close gently. Take three slow, deep breaths through the nose. Feel the weight of the body, the contact with the ground, the air on the skin. You have arrived — there is nowhere else to be.

4

Choose one anchor

Rest your attention on a single object: the sensation of the breath at the nostrils or belly, a mantra, a candle flame, or a feeling of loving-kindness. One anchor is enough — this is the home you will keep returning to.

5

Wander, notice, return

The mind will drift — to plans, memories, sounds. This is not failure; it is the moment of practice. The instant you notice you have wandered, gently, without scolding, guide attention back to the anchor. Do this a thousand times if needed.

6

Close with gratitude

When your time is complete, sit for a few breaths in the stillness you have made. Dedicate the practice — to your teachers, to all beings, to your own awakening. Open the eyes slowly and carry the calm into your day.

Start small

Beginners: 5–10 minutes

Begin with a length you can keep every day. Five honest minutes daily will carry you further than an hour once a week. Add a minute or two each week as it becomes natural — steadiness matters far more than duration.

Go deeper

Advanced: 30–60 minutes

Longer sittings allow the mind to drop below its surface chatter. Sit in two or three blocks, use a mala to structure japa, or extend into stillness without an anchor. Retreats and a trusted teacher deepen what daily practice begins.

A gentle note

Sitting with a busy mind is not a sign you are doing it wrong — it is what meditation is for. If strong emotions or unsettling experiences arise, meet them with kindness, shorten the sitting, and speak with a qualified teacher or counsellor. This page offers guidance, not medical or psychological treatment.

04

The unfolding

What Happens With Meditation

Nothing dramatic needs to happen — and yet much does. Here is what unfolds during a sitting, and what quietly ripens over time as practice deepens.

During a sitting

The body softens

Breathing slows and deepens, muscles release, and a pleasant heaviness or warmth spreads. Heart rate and blood pressure often ease. You may notice the body becoming still and light.

The mind slows

Thoughts do not vanish — their grip loosens. Gaps open between them: a quiet space, a stillness you can rest in. Sounds arrive without pulling you away.

Feelings surface

As the noise settles, buried emotions may rise — tenderness, joy, restlessness, old grief. Met with patience, they pass like weather. This clearing is part of the healing.

Over time — the long arc of sadhana

First days

Restlessness and doubt

The mind seems busier than ever and you wonder if you are 'doing it right'. This is normal — you are simply seeing the mind's ordinary activity for the first time. Keep sitting gently.

First weeks

A little more calm

Sittings begin to feel more settled. You notice small shifts off the cushion: a shorter fuse, better sleep, a moment of ease before reacting. The habit begins to take root.

First months

Steadiness and insight

Attention stays longer on the anchor. You begin to see your own patterns — how thoughts build, how emotions rise and fall — with growing objectivity and compassion. Concentration becomes a place you can return to at will.

A year and beyond

Absorption and equanimity

Practice becomes a refuge and a way of being. Deep states of absorption (dhyana) may open, and equanimity — a steady, unshaken evenness — colours daily life. The seeker and the seeking grow quieter, and peace is no longer something you chase but something you rest in.

Reported by practitioners

Everyday fruits

  • Calmer, more even moods and less reactivity
  • Deeper, more restful sleep
  • Sharper focus and clearer decisions
  • Greater patience, kindness and self-acceptance
  • A felt sense of connection and meaning

The contemplative view

What the traditions say

Yogic and Buddhist traditions describe meditation as a path of purification and awakening: the settling of mental turbulence (chitta vritti), the burning away of old impressions (samskaras), and ultimately the direct recognition of one's true nature. The fruits are both inner peace in this life and, in many traditions, liberation itself.

Honest cautions

Intensive practice can occasionally bring up difficult material — anxiety, vivid memories, or a sense of unreality. This is not a reason to abandon the path, but a sign to slow down, ground yourself in the body, keep sessions short, and seek an experienced teacher or mental-health professional if distress persists.

How the body sits

Postures & Silhouettes

A picture of the body helps the mind settle. These silhouettes show the classic ways to sit — choose the one your body can hold with ease.

Padmasana (Lotus)

Cross-legged with each foot resting on the opposite thigh. The classic, most stable seat for long sitting.

Sukhasana (Easy pose)

Simply cross-legged, comfortable and relaxed — ideal for beginners and for japa.

Chair / seated

Sitting on a chair, feet flat, spine upright. Perfect if sitting on the floor is difficult.

Vajrasana (Kneeling)

Kneeling with the seat on the heels. Keeps the back naturally straight and aids digestion.

Shavasana (Lying down)

Lying on the back, arms relaxed. Used for yoga nidra and body-scan practices.

Walking / with mala

Walking slowly with awareness, or standing with the mala held in the hand for japa.

Your first week

The Beginner Path — 7 Gentle Days

A simple, forgiving plan to build the habit. Ten minutes a day is plenty; the goal is to show up, not to perform.

Day 1–2

Just arrive

Sit for five minutes and simply follow the breath. No technique beyond noticing. Your only task is to show up.

Day 3–4

Add a count

Count breaths from one to ten, then begin again. This gives the restless mind a gentle job to do.

Day 5

Try japa

Repeat a simple mantra (or 'Om') with each breath, on a mala if you have one. Feel the rhythm.

Day 6

Lengthen slightly

Extend to ten minutes. Notice any resistance and sit with it kindly — it will pass.

Day 7

Reflect

After sitting, note one word for how you feel. This begins the diary of your own journey.

Then onward

Make it tap

Fix the time and place. What began as a week becomes a daily sadhana — your tap.

The library

Techniques of Meditation & Japa

Search or filter, then tap any card to open its method, ideal duration, benefits and cautions.

Anapanasati

Buddhist · breath awareness
Beginner

What it is

Mindfulness of breathing — the foundational practice of resting attention on the natural breath.

How to practise

  • Sit comfortably and let the breath be natural.
  • Feel the air at the nostrils or the rise and fall of the belly.
  • When the mind wanders, gently return to the breath.
  • Extend slowly, without strain.

Ideal duration

5–30 minutes

Benefits

Calm, concentration, present-moment awareness.

Caution

None. A perfect starting point for everyone.

Vipassana

Theravada Buddhist · insight
Intermediate

What it is

Insight meditation — observing sensations, thoughts and feelings rise and fall to see their impermanent nature.

How to practise

  • Begin with breath awareness to settle.
  • Scan the body, noting sensations without reacting.
  • Observe thoughts and feelings as passing events.
  • Rest in the growing clarity and equanimity.

Ideal duration

20–60 minutes

Benefits

Deep insight, emotional balance, release of reactivity.

Caution

Long retreats can surface strong material; practise with guidance.

Zazen

Zen · just sitting
Intermediate

What it is

The Zen practice of sitting in alert stillness, facing the wall, letting thoughts come and go without engagement.

How to practise

  • Sit in a stable, upright posture facing a wall.
  • Keep the eyes half-open, gaze lowered.
  • Let thoughts pass without following or fighting them.
  • Return to posture and breath, moment to moment.

Ideal duration

20–45 minutes

Benefits

Stability, simplicity, unshakeable presence.

Caution

Can feel austere at first; patience is needed.

Mantra Meditation / Japa

Vedic · devotional
Beginner

What it is

Repetition of a sacred sound — Om, the Gayatri mantra, or a name of the Divine — aloud or silently.

How to practise

  • Choose a mantra and sit comfortably.
  • Repeat it softly with each breath.
  • Use a mala to keep count if you wish.
  • Let the sound grow subtle and silent within.

Ideal duration

10–30 minutes

Benefits

Focused mind, devotion, inner warmth.

Caution

Choose a mantra that feels sacred and true to you.

Ajapa Japa

Vedic · spontaneous repetition
Advanced

What it is

The state in which mantra repetition continues by itself, silently, without conscious effort — the breath itself becomes the mantra.

How to practise

  • Establish steady japa for a long period.
  • Let the rhythm sync with the natural breath.
  • Release the effort to repeat; let it repeat itself.
  • Rest as the silent witness of the sound.

Ideal duration

30–60 minutes

Benefits

Deep absorption, effortless presence.

Caution

Best approached after a settled, regular japa practice.

Trataka

Hatha Yoga · gazing
Intermediate

What it is

Steady gazing at a single object — usually a candle flame — to gather and still the mind.

How to practise

  • Place a candle at eye level, a metre away.
  • Gaze softly at the flame without blinking as long as comfortable.
  • Close the eyes and hold the after-image.
  • Repeat, then rest in the darkness.

Ideal duration

10–20 minutes

Benefits

Concentration, inner vision, mental clarity.

Caution

Not for those prone to eye strain, headaches or visual sensitivity.

Yoga Nidra

Yogic · guided sleep-awareness
Beginner

What it is

A guided practice of lying down in deep relaxation while remaining awake and aware — 'yogic sleep'.

How to practise

  • Lie in shavasana, warm and still.
  • Follow a voice through the body, breath and imagery.
  • Stay awake yet deeply relaxed.
  • Return slowly, carrying the calm with you.

Ideal duration

20–45 minutes

Benefits

Profound rest, stress release, better sleep.

Caution

You may fall asleep — that is rest too; simply begin again.

Metta / Loving-kindness

Buddhist · heart practice
Beginner

What it is

Cultivating goodwill — first toward yourself, then loved ones, then all beings.

How to practise

  • Sit and bring a loved one or yourself to mind.
  • Silently offer wishes: 'May you be happy, may you be safe.'
  • Extend gradually to strangers and difficult people.
  • Let the warmth spread to all beings everywhere.

Ideal duration

10–30 minutes

Benefits

Compassion, healing, emotional warmth.

Caution

Difficult emotions may surface; meet them with kindness.

Chakra & Kundalini Meditation

Tantric · energy centres
Advanced

What it is

Working with the body's energy centres and the rising of kundalini along the spine.

How to practise

  • Sit upright and ground the body.
  • Bring awareness to each chakra from root to crown.
  • Use breath, mantra and visualisation to purify each centre.
  • Rest in the stillness at the crown.

Ideal duration

30–60 minutes

Benefits

Vitality, depth, transformative insight.

Caution

Traditionally practised under an experienced teacher; proceed gently.

Walking Meditation

Buddhist · movement
Beginner

What it is

Meditation in motion — bringing the same gentle awareness to each step.

How to practise

  • Walk slowly on a quiet path.
  • Feel each foot lift, move and touch the ground.
  • Let the breath and steps fall into rhythm.
  • When the mind wanders, return to the feet.

Ideal duration

10–30 minutes

Benefits

Grounding, energy, awareness in daily life.

Caution

Choose a safe, level place free of traffic.

Sound / Nada Yoga

Yogic · sound
Intermediate

What it is

Deep listening — resting attention on inner or outer sound until the mind merges with it.

How to practise

  • Sit in a quiet place and close the eyes.
  • Listen to the sounds around you without labelling them.
  • Turn the attention inward to the subtle inner sound.
  • Let awareness dissolve into the sound.

Ideal duration

15–40 minutes

Benefits

Deep calm, sensitivity, absorption.

Caution

Use comfortable, non-startling sound levels.

Gratitude & Self-enquiry

Vedantic · Atma Vichara
Advanced

What it is

Resting in the question 'Who am I?' — turning attention back to its own source.

How to practise

  • Sit and let the mind settle.
  • Ask sincerely: 'Who am I?'
  • Do not answer with thought; rest in the sense of being.
  • Return to the question whenever the mind stirs.

Ideal duration

20–45 minutes

Benefits

Self-knowledge, peace, freedom from identification.

Caution

Subtle practice; a teacher's guidance is valuable.

Mantra & discipline

Japa & Tap

Japa is the repetition of a sacred sound; tap is the steady discipline that gives it power. Together they form the beating heart of devotional practice — simple to begin, endless to deepen.

How to practise japa

  • Hold the mala in your right hand, draped over the middle finger, moved with the thumb.
  • Begin at the bead after the guru bead (the larger, often tasselled bead) and move bead by bead.
  • Repeat your mantra aloud, whispered, or silently with each bead — matching it to the breath if it helps.
  • Complete 108 beads for one round, then turn back without crossing the guru bead.
  • Use a personal mantra received from a teacher if you have one; otherwise 'Om', the Gayatri mantra, or a name of the Divine.

Building tap

  • Same time, same place, every day. Regularity is the secret of tap.
  • Set a sankalpa — a clear, heartfelt resolve, such as 'one mala each morning'.
  • Start small and keep it. A steady small count outlasts a heroic one.
  • Offer it up. Dedicate the practice to something larger than yourself.
  • Practise with joy, not punishment. Tap is warmth, not self-harm.

Questions on the path

Wisdom & FAQ

Neither is superior; they suit different temperaments. Japa gives the mind a sacred sound to hold and often suits devotional seekers. Silent meditation cultivates bare awareness and insight. Many practitioners combine both, using japa to settle the mind before sitting in stillness.

A light, sattvic diet, regular sleep and a modest, ethical life make the mind easier to settle — they are the ground of practice. No strict regimen is required to begin, but living gently does support sitting gently.

Calm and steadiness often appear within weeks of daily practice; deeper insight ripens over months and years. The surest way to progress is simply to keep the habit. Progress is measured in consistency, not intensity.
